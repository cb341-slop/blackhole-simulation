Equations: body
r_{\mathrm s}=\frac{2GM}{c^2}

M is mass; G is the gravitational constant; c is the speed of light.

\chi=\frac{Jc}{GM^2}

J is black-hole angular momentum. Zero spin gives Schwarzschild; nonzero spin selects Kerr.

r_+=\frac{r_{\mathrm s}}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-\chi^2}\right)

Kerr horizon radius in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates. At zero spin, r₊ = rₛ.

f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{Q^2}{r^2}

Charged, nonrotating Reissner–Nordström metric factor. Here G = c = 1; Q/M > 1 has no horizon.

Sources: Carroll, Lecture Notes on General Relativity, §7; Chan et al., GRay2, §II

Normal overlay notes

Hover or tap the lensed (left) image. The selected disk/horizon hit is projected to its actual position in the straight view; both arrows use the same source point, face, and world normal. The lensed arrow direction comes from a neighboring-ray bundle, including image rotation and parity reversal. The straight arrow uses perspective projection. Arrow lengths are normalized for readability; ⊙ / ⊗ show the normal component toward / away from the ray, and ? marks an unavailable projection near a caustic or trace boundary. Dashed arrows mark points hidden by the horizon, cubes, or the back-facing surface. Off-screen points are reported in the readout. Hovering the right image keeps the last lensed preview. A click or tap on the disk pins its fixed XYZ source point; both markers then reproject that point as you move the camera. The pin stays when the pointer leaves, and a new disk click replaces it. Drags and multi-touch gestures only move the camera. Clear pin restores hover inspection. If the selected image branch disappears or moves off-screen, the source point is retained and reported. This images a small virtual coordinate-normal marker; it does not parallel-transport a spacetime vector. Method background: Perlick, §2.3, light-ray bundles. Normal and projection source.

Equations: light paths
\frac{d k^\mu}{d\lambda}=-\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}k^\alpha k^\beta

Geodesic equation. k is the tangent to a light ray; λ is an affine parameter; Γ describes the connection of the selected spacetime.

g_{\mu\nu}k^\mu k^\nu=0

Light rays are null. The solver traces them backward from the camera.

\alpha\simeq\frac{4GM}{bc^2},\qquad b\gg r_{\mathrm s}

Weak-field Schwarzschild limit only. α is the deflection angle and b is the impact parameter at infinity. The strong-field image uses integrated geodesics, not this approximation.

Equations: circular orbits
\dot r^2=E^2-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(1+\frac{L^2}{r^2}\right)

Schwarzschild reference (χ = 0), in G = c = 1. E and L are specific energy and angular momentum; the dot is a proper-time derivative. Nonzero spin uses the Kerr equations.

Model & controls

A naked singularity is a theoretical spacetime singularity without an event horizon. The uncharged black-hole mode uses Schwarzschild at zero spin (horizon r = 2M), or Kerr when χ > 0. The naked-singularity mode uses the charged, nonrotating Reissner–Nordström solution with Q/M > 1, in geometric units G = c = 1.

f(r)=1-2M/r+Q^2/r^2

Each pixel follows a numerically integrated null geodesic. An inward gravitational term and an outward charge term bend its path. For 1 < Q/M ≤ √(9/8), photon spheres can still exist; above that threshold there are no circular photon orbits.

The glowing disk is an artistic, optically thin emitter. It is not a self-consistent accretion model. Cubes are static coordinate-space markers. Finite integration steps and a small central cutoff limit accuracy close to the singularity; this is an exploratory visualization, not a research solver.

Light paths & debug geometry

Choose “Light paths” in the View selector to orbit an external view of rays traced backward from the observer. Bright moving markers illustrate the paths; they are not a coordinate-time animation. Geometry toggles show the center, grid, camera frustum, Schwarzschild reference sphere, and photon spheres. The Schwarzschild radius is a true horizon only in the black-hole mode.

Disk coordinates & comparison

Enable Disk UV debug to match labeled disk regions A1–H4 to the source map. U wraps around the disk; V runs from inner to outer radius. TOP (+Y) uses warm colors; BOTTOM (−Y) uses cool colors. The UV view is an opaque first-intersection diagnostic. Its grid can stay fixed or follow the disk rotation. Compare lensing shows the same observer, time, and scene with curved rays on the left and straight rays on the right. Both views move and pause together.

Ray diagnostics

Render diagnostics show integration steps, closest approach, net direction change (not accumulated orbit angle), disk crossings, or termination. These traces continue through disk emission; an opaque cube still ends the GPU ray. Red budget pixels mean the ray did not finish within the selected step count. The cursor inspector runs a separate, finer CPU reference geodesic. Its first-hit disk coordinates and outcome can differ near sharp boundaries or strongly bent paths. The reference reports energy drift for Schwarzschild/RN, or the normalized null-constraint residual |2ℋ| for Kerr. These are numerical consistency checks, not a bound on image accuracy.

Falling into a black hole

The descent is a cinematic camera animation with an accelerating radial profile. The final image fades at the event horizon, where the static-observer rendering ends. It is not a physically calibrated free-fall clock or an interior black-hole simulation. Pause, stop, reset, or replay at any time.

Rotating black holes, horizon UV & orbits

Nonzero black-hole spin selects Kerr null geodesics, traced backward in outgoing Cartesian Kerr–Schild coordinates. The static camera stays outside the ergosphere. The horizon radius is r₊ = M(1 + √(1 − χ²)); the disk inner edge follows the prograde ISCO. UV debug colors captured rays by artificial horizon longitude/latitude and can rotate at ΩH. These labels are coordinate markers, not horizon radiation. The Kerr shadow’s displacement and distortion come from the light trajectories.

The orbit lab integrates equatorial timelike Kerr geodesics in proper time using conserved energy and angular momentum. Circular-orbit stability is classified by the chosen prograde/retrograde photon radius and ISCO. Perturbations recompute energy to satisfy the radial constraint. Trajectories stop just outside the horizon. The enlarged coordinate diagram and finite ray integration are educational; radiation reaction, an accretion flow solution, and interior motion are not included. Existing spatial-curvature and tidal views select Schwarzschild (zero spin).

Spatial curvature & tidal stretching

Curvature shows Flamm’s embedding of the Schwarzschild exterior equatorial spatial metric: dz/dr = √(2M/(r − 2M)). The outer edge is translated to height zero. Scale 1 preserves the spatial metric; other vertical scales are display changes. The diagram contains no interior and does not represent all of four-dimensional spacetime.

Spaghettification follows a radial E = 1 geodesic from 20M to 2.2M. For x = r/20M, the linear radial and transverse separation factors are (x² + 4/√x)/5 and 2√x − x, initialized with zero relative velocity. These solve the freely falling frame’s geodesic-deviation equations. A non-interacting particle cloud stretches radially and contracts in both transverse directions. Cloud size only enlarges the diagram; strain ratios are unchanged. The centre stops outside the horizon. Enlarged points may overlap its wireframe and do not indicate particles crossing the horizon.
